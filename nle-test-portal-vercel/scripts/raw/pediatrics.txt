Q1. A 4-day-old male newborn develops progressive abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, and failure to pass stool. He was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy. On examination, the abdomen is markedly distended, and digital rectal examination is followed by explosive passage of stool and gas. Contrast enema demonstrates a narrow distal segment with proximal colonic dilatation. Which of the following is the most appropriate definitive management?
A. Immediate ileostomy without further evaluation
B. Rectal suction biopsy followed by definitive pull-through surgery if confirmed
C. Pyloromyotomy followed by enteral feeding
D. Ladd procedure with division of Ladd bands
E. Conservative management with repeated enemas
Answer: B.
Explanation: Hirschsprung disease results from failure of neural crest cell migration, producing an aganglionic distal bowel segment. The classic presentation is delayed meconium passage, abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, and explosive stool after rectal examination. Rectal suction biopsy confirms absence of ganglion cells, while definitive treatment is a pull-through procedure such as Soave, Swenson, or Duhamel.

Q2. A 7-week-old male infant is brought because of progressively worsening projectile vomiting after feeds. The vomitus is non-bilious, and the infant remains hungry immediately after vomiting. He has lost weight and appears mildly dehydrated. Laboratory testing demonstrates hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. A small, firm, mobile mass is palpated in the epigastrium. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?
A. Immediate Ramstedt pyloromyotomy without correction of electrolytes
B. Intravenous fluid and electrolyte correction followed by Ramstedt pyloromyotomy
C. Ladd procedure with correction of intestinal malrotation
D. Nasogastric feeding followed by observation
E. Broad-spectrum antibiotics followed by laparotomy
Answer: B.
Explanation: Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis classically presents at 2–8 weeks, with projectile non-bilious vomiting and persistent hunger. The characteristic biochemical abnormality is hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. Surgery is Ramstedt pyloromyotomy, but dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities must be corrected first.

Q3. A 3-month-old infant presents with recurrent episodes of sudden crying, drawing the legs toward the abdomen, and intermittent lethargy. Between episodes, the infant appears relatively comfortable. During one episode, vomiting occurs, and later the parents notice blood mixed with mucus in the diaper. Abdominal examination reveals a sausage-shaped mass in the right upper quadrant. Ultrasound demonstrates a characteristic target or doughnut sign. Which intervention is most appropriate in a hemodynamically stable child without evidence of perforation?
A. Immediate laparotomy in every case
B. Pneumatic or hydrostatic enema reduction
C. Oral rehydration followed by observation
D. Ramstedt pyloromyotomy
E. Antibiotics alone until symptoms resolve
Answer: B.
Explanation: This is intussusception, commonly occurring between 6 months and 3 years, although it can occur in younger infants. The classic triad of episodic abdominal pain, palpable sausage-shaped mass, and currant-jelly stool is often incomplete. In a stable child without perforation or peritonitis, air or contrast enema is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Q4. A premature infant develops increasing abdominal distension, feeding intolerance, lethargy, and bloody stools on the 12th day of life. Abdominal radiography demonstrates dilated bowel loops and multiple linear or bubbly collections of gas within the intestinal wall. The infant becomes increasingly unstable despite initial supportive therapy. Which finding would most strongly indicate progression to a surgical emergency?
A. Mild abdominal distension
B. Feeding intolerance alone
C. Pneumatosis intestinalis without systemic deterioration
D. Pneumoperitoneum indicating intestinal perforation
E. Small amounts of occult blood in stool
Answer: D.
Explanation: Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is particularly associated with premature infants. Pneumatosis intestinalis is highly characteristic. Initial management includes bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids, antibiotics, and parenteral nutrition. Pneumoperitoneum indicates perforation and is an indication for surgical intervention.

Q5. A 5-year-old child presents with fever, severe sore throat, drooling, muffled speech, and increasing respiratory distress. He prefers to sit upright and refuses to lie down. Which organism is classically associated with this presentation in an inadequately immunized child?
A. Streptococcus pneumoniae
B. Haemophilus influenzae type b
C. Mycoplasma pneumoniae
D. Bordetella pertussis
E. Corynebacterium diphtheriae
Answer: B.
Explanation: Acute epiglottitis classically presents with high fever, drooling, dysphagia, muffled voice, tripod positioning, and rapidly progressive airway obstruction. Historically, H. influenzae type b was the major cause, although vaccination has dramatically reduced its incidence. Airway management is the priority; aggressive throat examination should be avoided.

Q6. A 2-year-old child develops barking cough, hoarseness, and inspiratory stridor that becomes worse at night. He has a low-grade fever but does not appear toxic. Q8. A newborn develops respiratory distress shortly after birth. He was delivered at 34 weeks by cesarean section and has tachypnea, nasal flaring, grunting, and intercostal retractions. Explanation: Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) occurs primarily in premature infants because type II pneumocytes have inadequate surfactant production. Q9. A 9-month-old infant is brought because of poor growth, recurrent respiratory infections, and sweating during feeds. Examination reveals tachypnea and a harsh holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border. Echocardiography demonstrates a defect between the ventricles with significant left-to-right shunting. Which complication is most likely if a large defect remains untreated for years?
A. Immediate severe cyanosis at birth
B. Eisenmenger syndrome due to progressive pulmonary vascular disease
C. Tetralogy of Fallot
D. Complete heart block in every patient
E. Severe pulmonary stenosis caused by ductal closure
Answer: B.
Explanation: A significant ventricular septal defect (VSD) produces a left-to-right shunt, increasing pulmonary blood flow. Over time, pulmonary vascular resistance may rise, eventually reversing the shunt and causing cyanosis—Eisenmenger syndrome. Large VSDs can cause heart failure and recurrent respiratory infections during infancy.

Q10. A cyanotic newborn becomes increasingly hypoxemic shortly after birth. Q11. A 4-year-old child has fever, pallor, easy bruising, and generalized lymphadenopathy. Q12. A 3-year-old child presents with abdominal distension and a large, firm abdominal mass that does not cross the midline. Urinalysis shows microscopic hematuria. Q14. A 18-month-old child is brought with progressive difficulty walking and frequent falls. Examination reveals widened wrists, bowing of the legs, delayed dentition, and hypotonia. Q15. A 4-year-old child develops generalized edema, especially around the eyes, after an upper respiratory infection. Urinalysis shows heavy proteinuria without significant hematuria. Q17. A 10-year-old child develops palpable purpura over both lower limbs and buttocks following an upper respiratory infection. He subsequently develops colicky abdominal pain and arthralgia. Explanation: Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) commonly occurs in obese adolescents. The adolescent may present with hip or referred knee pain and an externally rotated limb. The affected epiphysis must be stabilized with surgical fixation to prevent further displacement and avascular necrosis.

Q87. A 9-year-old child develops a painful swollen knee and ankle several weeks after a diarrheal illness. The child has conjunctival irritation and dysuria. No organisms are recovered from joint aspiration. Which diagnosis is most likely?
A. Septic arthritis
B. Reactive arthritis
C. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis
D. Hemophilic arthropathy
E. Osteomyelitis
Answer: B.
Explanation: Reactive arthritis occurs after certain gastrointestinal or genitourinary infections. The classic clinical pattern includes arthritis with conjunctivitis and urethritis. The joint itself is sterile because the inflammation is an immune-mediated response rather than direct infection.

Q88. A 4-year-old child has persistent fever, rash, and swelling of multiple joints for more than 6 weeks. Laboratory studies reveal elevated inflammatory markers and anemia of chronic disease. No infectious cause is identified. Which diagnosis should be considered?
A. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis
B. Acute appendicitis
C. Hemolytic uremic syndrome
D. Kawasaki disease only
E. Osteomyelitis exclusively
Answer: A.
Explanation: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is chronic arthritis beginning before age 16 and persisting for at least 6 weeks after exclusion of other causes. Systemic JIA may cause quotidian fever and an evanescent rash. Uveitis is an important extra-articular complication in selected subtypes.

Q89. A 5-year-old child has chronic pallor and fatigue. CBC demonstrates microcytic, hypochromic anemia. Dietary history reveals excessive cow's milk consumption with limited iron-rich foods. Which laboratory pattern is most consistent with iron-deficiency anemia?
A. High ferritin and low TIBC
B. Low ferritin and increased TIBC
C. High ferritin and high serum iron
D. Normal ferritin with markedly increased vitamin B12
E. Increased transferrin saturation
Answer: B.
Explanation: Iron-deficiency anemia is characterized by low serum ferritin, low serum iron, increased total iron-binding capacity, and reduced transferrin saturation. In children, excessive cow's milk consumption and inadequate dietary iron are common contributors.

Q90. A 7-year-old child presents with fatigue, pallor, bruising, fever, and bone pain. CBC shows anemia and thrombocytopenia. Peripheral smear reveals blasts. Bone marrow examination confirms acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Which complication is particularly important before initiating certain treatments because rapid tumor destruction can produce metabolic abnormalities?
A. Tumor lysis syndrome
B. Scurvy
C. Rickets
D. Achalasia
E. Croup
Answer: A.
Explanation: Tumor lysis syndrome can occur spontaneously or after chemotherapy, especially in high-burden malignancies such as ALL. Rapid cell destruction releases potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids, causing hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and hyperuricemia, potentially leading to acute kidney injury.

Q91. A 3-year-old child develops fever, pallor, petechiae, and generalized lymphadenopathy. Bone marrow examination reveals lymphoblasts. The oncology team performs lumbar puncture as part of staging and prophylactic management. Why is central nervous system-directed therapy important in this malignancy?
A. Leukemic cells cannot enter the CNS
B. The CNS can serve as a sanctuary site for leukemic cells
C. CNS treatment prevents iron deficiency
D. It prevents bacterial pneumonia only
E. It is required only for solid tumors
Answer: B.
Explanation: In ALL, leukemic cells may persist within the CNS because many systemic chemotherapeutic agents have limited penetration across the blood-brain barrier. Therefore, intrathecal chemotherapy is an important component of treatment and helps reduce CNS relapse.

Q92. A 2-year-old child is found to have a large abdominal mass. Imaging reveals a renal tumor, and genetic evaluation demonstrates a deletion involving a tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 11. Which syndrome is classically associated with increased risk of this tumor along with aniridia?
A. WAGR syndrome
B. Turner syndrome
C. Marfan syndrome
D. DiGeorge syndrome
E. Klinefelter syndrome
Answer: A.
Explanation: WAGR syndrome consists of Wilms tumor, aniridia, genitourinary abnormalities, and intellectual/developmental disability. It is associated with abnormalities involving 11p13, including the WT1 region. Wilms tumor risk is increased in several congenital syndromes.

Q93. A newborn has a midline abdominal wall defect through which abdominal viscera protrude. The organs are covered by a translucent membrane. Which congenital anomaly is being described?
A. Gastroschisis
B. Omphalocele
C. Diaphragmatic hernia
D. Meckel diverticulum
E. Umbilical hernia
Answer: B.
Explanation: Omphalocele is a congenital midline abdominal wall defect in which herniated abdominal contents are covered by a membrane. It is frequently associated with other congenital abnormalities, particularly cardiac and chromosomal disorders. Gastroschisis, in contrast, is usually paraumbilical and lacks a covering membrane.

Q94. A newborn is found to have bowel protruding through a defect immediately lateral to the umbilicus. The exposed bowel is not covered by a membrane. Which complication is particularly concerning because of direct exposure of the bowel to amniotic fluid?
A. Bowel inflammation and fluid/electrolyte losses
B. Immediate development of pyloric stenosis
C. Hyperthyroidism
D. Nephrotic syndrome
E. Isolated hemolytic anemia
Answer: A.
Explanation: Gastroschisis involves uncovered bowel protruding through an abdominal wall defect, usually to the right of the umbilicus. Exposure causes bowel inflammation, edema, heat and fluid loss, and potential ischemic complications. Surgical protection and repair are required.

Q95. A newborn develops abdominal distension and bilious vomiting. Imaging demonstrates a “double-bubble” appearance with little gas distal to the obstruction. The infant also has features suggesting Down syndrome. Which diagnosis is most likely?
A. Duodenal atresia
B. Pyloric stenosis
C. Hirschsprung disease
D. Intussusception
E. Meconium ileus
Answer: A.
Explanation: Duodenal atresia classically produces early bilious vomiting and a double-bubble sign on abdominal radiography. It is strongly associated with trisomy 21. Surgical repair is required after stabilization.

Q96. A 2-week-old infant has persistent vomiting and poor feeding. The vomitus is non-bilious, and the infant remains hungry after vomiting. Ultrasound confirms thickening of the pyloric muscle. Which acid-base abnormality is classically associated with this disorder?
A. Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis
B. Hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis
C. Respiratory acidosis
D. Hyperkalemic metabolic alkalosis
E. Lactic acidosis without electrolyte abnormalities
Answer: B.
Explanation: Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis causes repeated loss of gastric hydrochloric acid through vomiting. This produces hypochloremia and metabolic alkalosis; potassium depletion may develop. The infant should undergo fluid/electrolyte correction before Ramstedt pyloromyotomy.

Q97. A 5-year-old child has poor growth, chronic cough, recurrent sinus and lung infections, and steatorrhea. Sweat chloride testing is positive. The child is started on pancreatic enzyme replacement. Which additional nutritional deficiency is particularly likely without appropriate supplementation?
A. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K
B. Vitamin C only
C. Vitamin B12 only
D. Folate only
E. Iron only
Answer: A.
Explanation: Cystic fibrosis causes exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, resulting in impaired digestion and absorption of fats and fat-soluble vitamins. Supplementation with A, D, E, and K is therefore commonly required, along with pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Q98. A 4-year-old child is brought for evaluation because he cannot see well at night and has difficulty adapting to dim environments. Examination demonstrates conjunctival dryness and foamy Bitot spots. His diet is severely deficient in animal products and colorful vegetables. Which deficiency is most likely responsible?
A. Vitamin A deficiency
B. Vitamin C deficiency
C. Vitamin D deficiency
D. Vitamin K deficiency
E. Vitamin B1 deficiency
Answer: A.
Explanation: Vitamin A deficiency causes impaired dark adaptation and can progress to xerophthalmia, Bitot spots, corneal ulceration, and blindness. Vitamin A is essential for retinal photopigment function and maintenance of normal epithelial surfaces.

Q99. A 3-year-old child presents with bleeding gums, painful swollen joints, and multiple perifollicular hemorrhages. The diet consists almost entirely of processed foods with very little fruit or vegetables. Which biochemical process is impaired in this condition?
A. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine during collagen synthesis
B. γ-carboxylation of clotting factors
C. Conversion of bilirubin to urobilinogen
D. Synthesis of surfactant phospholipids
E. Oxidative phosphorylation in skeletal muscle only
Answer: A.
Explanation: Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) impairs collagen synthesis because vitamin C is required for hydroxylation of proline and lysine. Children may develop gingival bleeding, perifollicular hemorrhage, bone pain, poor wound healing, and anemia.

Q100. A 6-year-old child is brought for routine evaluation. His height and weight have consistently tracked along the same percentiles, he performs age-appropriate activities, and examination is normal. The parents are concerned because he is shorter than several classmates. His mother is 155 cm tall and his father is 165 cm tall. There are no systemic symptoms or signs of endocrine disease. Which explanation is most likely?
A. Familial short stature
B. Growth hormone deficiency
C. Hypothyroidism
D. Cushing syndrome
E. Chronic inflammatory disease
Answer: A.
Explanation: Familial short stature is characterized by short parents, normal growth velocity, and height tracking along a lower but appropriate percentile. Bone age is generally consistent with chronological age. In contrast, endocrine disease usually causes reduced growth velocity and progressive crossing downward of growth percentiles.
